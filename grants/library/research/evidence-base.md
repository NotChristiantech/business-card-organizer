# Evidence base — citable statistics

Everything a needs statement requires, in one place. **Figures below are drawn
from ACBN's own summaries of the source reports, not from the PDFs directly.**
Open the source before quoting a number in a submitted application.

## The financing gap

- **75% of Black entrepreneurs report difficulty securing even $10,000** in
  funding — Abacus Data pan-Canadian survey, commissioned by the African
  Canadian Senate Group and Senator Colin Deacon.

  *The single best statistic in this library.* It is national, it is
  Senate-commissioned, and $10,000 is small enough that the number lands
  viscerally in a way "access to capital is a barrier" never does. It also sits
  directly beneath ACBN's microloan range of $500–$25,000 — the program is
  precisely sized to the documented gap. Pair the two.

- Systemic racism affects Black entrepreneurs across three specific channels:
  **access to capital, access to networks and mentorship, and trust in
  financial institutions** (Inclusive Entrepreneurship / Abacus).
- Black entrepreneurs hold **limited trust in institutions, particularly banks**
  — a demand-side barrier that persists even where supply-side programs exist.
- Black entrepreneurs are predominantly **young, new to business, reliant on
  bootstrapping, and uncomfortable discussing funding with financial
  institutions** (Building Black Businesses in Canada — CBCC / Pitch Better / BDC).

## Structure of Black-owned business in Canada

- A significant share are **nano and micro-enterprises with fewer than five
  employees** — which is why microloans and simplified applications matter more
  than large instruments.
- ACBN's own survey found **71% either unregistered or sole proprietors** and
  **70% in traditional service-based, low-tech companies**
  (see `acbn-own-research.md`).

## The "empowered vs. unempowered" frame

From the Abacus / Senate work. Empowered Black entrepreneurs have strong support
networks, confidence in responding to challenges, access to capital and the
ability to recover from setbacks; unempowered entrepreneurs lack all four.

Useful because it reframes ACBN's work as *moving entrepreneurs between two
documented states* rather than delivering activities — which is exactly the shape
of an outcome a funder wants to buy.

## Federal program landscape

- **Black Entrepreneurship Program (BEP)**, launched 2020:
  - **Black Entrepreneurship Loan Fund (BELF)** — loans up to $250,000
  - **National Ecosystem Fund** — $53M for Black-led business support orgs
  - **Black Entrepreneurship Knowledge Hub** — $5M for data and research
- **SBCCI**, established 2019 — capacity for Black-led organisations.
- **BELF's lending agreement expires 2025**; advocates recommend the program be
  made permanent and substantially increased.
- **Black Opportunity Fund** — targeting a $1.5B fund generating $90–125M
  annually, to support 450+ community organisations and 8,000 Black businesses.

Knowing the program architecture matters: ACBN has drawn from the National
Ecosystem Fund ($1.315M) and SBCCI, so it can speak about these instruments as a
participant rather than an applicant.

## The data-gap argument

Every source in this folder repeats it: **there is no adequate disaggregated data
on Black-owned businesses and Black workers in Canada.**

This is ACBN's most strategically useful finding, because ACBN is one of the few
organisations actively closing it — a 1,014-respondent survey and a labour market
assessment conducted with the Ontario Public Service. When a funder asks what is
missing in the sector, ACBN can name the gap *and* point to its own work
addressing it. Very few applicants can do both.

## Documented policy recommendations

Useful when an application asks what systemic change is needed — these are
positions ACBN can align with, several of which ACBN co-authored:

- Make the BEP permanent with an increased budget
- Require KPIs in federal entrepreneurship and economic development programs
- Reform government procurement to reach underrepresented entrepreneurs
- Capitalise on diasporan connections for trade with African and Caribbean countries
- Consumer-driven (open) banking, to help Black entrepreneurs build credit history
- **Ontario specifically:** return to proactive employment equity (the Employment
  Equity Act was repealed in 1995), partner with Black data firms, collect
  disaggregated race and ethnicity data, and **fund Black-owned businesses to
  access professional services including grant writers**

That last one is worth noticing: ACBN is advocating for funding for exactly the
service ACBN provides. Fine to advance, but let it be a deliberate choice.

## Historical grounding

For narrative openings, where a funder's brief invites context:

- **Mathieu de Coste** — interpreter, considered the first Black entrepreneur in Canada
- **John Ware** — successful rancher in Alberta
- **The Blackburns** — Toronto taxi business, instrumental in the Underground Railroad

Black entrepreneurship in Canada is four centuries old, not a diversity
initiative. Used once, briefly, this reframes the entire application.

## Anti-racism policy anchors

- Canada's Anti-Racism Strategy (2019–2022)
- Ontario's Anti-Racism Act (2017) and Anti-Racism Strategic Plan
- Toronto Action Plan to Confront Anti-Black Racism

Cite the funder's own jurisdiction back to them — municipal funders respond to
the Toronto plan, provincial to the Anti-Racism Act.
